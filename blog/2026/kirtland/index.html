kirtland's warbler

Created on June 07, 2026

2026   ·   birds   travel

The Kirtland's Warbler (Setophaga kirtlandii) is a small threatened songbird with a very peculiar range: it winters in the Bahamas and only breeds in Northern Michigan and Wisconsin. This limited distribution is caused by its limiting nesting requirements, as the species requires pure stands of young Jack Pine (Pinus banksiana). Kirtland's Warblers are ground nesters and use the dense, low branches of Jack Pines to conceal their nest. As the trees get taller, the lower branches are lost and the patch becomes unusable to the warblers.

Despite their low numbers, Kirtland's Warblers are pretty easy to find if you know where to go. Some members of my cohort and I traveled up to Roscommon, Michigan (the states first bird city, see right), which is home to the annual Kirtland's Warbler festival.

At the festival, we paid $5 each and hopped on a red-and-green tourist trolley that took us east into the Huron National Forest. This was my first time Up North since moving to Michigan the prior summer, and the piney forests reminded me strongly of home. On the trolley, our guide pointed out a tall stand of Jack Pines and informed us that it was a Kirtland’s Warbler site in prior years, but was now too tall for the birds.

Left: Boarding the trolley to the warbler habitat! Right: Jack Pine stands in the Huron National Forest.

Since Kirtland’s Warblers need young Jack Pine, human fire supression threatened the species in the 20th century as it allowed the Jack Pines to grow too fast and prevented their serotinous cones from germinating. Conservation efforts helped establish a rotating timber harvesting system, which allows some patches to always be suitable for the Kirtland’s Warbler.

Upon arriving, we had a tough time spotting the birds. They are quite noisy and males frequently sing territorially, but the habitat was quite dense and the birds were often low to the ground. Eventually, we were able to get eyes on a few individuals, although all were backlit and difficult to make out.

Left: The Kirtland's Warbler breeding habitat where we saw the birds. It reminded me a lot of young Ponderosa Pine (Pinus ponderosa) stands in central Oregon. Right: The best photograph I managed to obtain of the warblers! I promise that is a Kirtland's Warbler.

After returning to Roscommon, we were given hand-signed certificates from the guide as proof of our sightining and mementos of the adventure. On our return to Lansing, we stopped outside of Houghton Lake (the largest lake by surface area entirely within Michigan) to see the breeding habitat of another bird, the Black Tern (Chlidonias niger).

Left: Posing with our Kirtland's Warbler sighting certificates! Right: A Black Tern!

An interesting piece of Kirtland’s Warbler trivia: Nathan Leopold (of Leopold and Loeb) was an ornithologist and helped provide some of the first documentation of Kirtland’s Warbler nesting sites.
